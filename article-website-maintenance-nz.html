Who looks after my website after it launches?

By the Tova Digital team · 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Whoever built your site isn't automatically looking after it. Hosting, updates, backups, security, edits and improvements each need a named owner, and that should be agreed in writing before you sign. Ask your provider who does each job, how quickly they respond, and whether you can take your site and data with you.

Launch day feels like the finish line. The site is live, the photos look great and someone has finally sent the link to your mum.

Then a few months pass. A plugin needs updating, a contact form stops sending, or you want to change your opening hours, and you realise you're not sure who to ask. That's a very common spot to be in, and it's easy to avoid if you sort it out early.

This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. It explains what looking after a website really involves, who is usually responsible, and what to ask before you sign anything.

What does "looking after" a website actually involve?

Looking after a website means six ongoing jobs: hosting, updates, backups, security, edits and improvements. Someone needs to own each one, whether that's you, your provider or another company.

Think of it like a building. The site is the shopfront, but there's also the land it sits on, the locks on the door and the person who changes the window display. Skip any of those and problems turn up eventually.

The trouble is that these jobs are mostly invisible when they're done well. You only notice them when they're missing, usually at an awkward moment.

Who is responsible for hosting?

Hosting is the service that keeps your website online, and it's the job of whoever you or your provider pay for it. That could be your web studio, a separate hosting company, or you directly.

In plain words, a host is a computer that's always on, always connected, and ready to show your site to anyone who visits. Good hosting means your site loads quickly and stays up. Poor hosting means slow pages and outages that cost you enquiries.

The key thing is knowing whose account it's in. Ask who pays the hosting bill, who can log in, and who to call when the site goes down. Your domain name (your web address) is a separate item and should be registered in your name or your business's name. If it isn't, you can have a hard time proving the site is yours.

Who keeps the website updated and secure?

Updates and security are the most overlooked jobs, and they should belong to someone technical who is paid to do them. Software behind a website, including the content management system (the tool that lets you edit pages) and any add-ons, needs regular updates.

Those updates often fix security holes. Leave them for too long and your site becomes an easier target, and a hacked site can mean lost customers, a warning from Google and a stressful clean-up.

Good security care usually covers:

  • Keeping the site's software and add-ons up to date.
  • Using secure connections, so the padlock shows in the browser.
  • Strong, unique logins, with extra checks where possible.
  • Watching for problems, so someone notices before your customers do.
  • Removing add-ons you no longer use.

Some sites need far less of this than others. A simple static site has fewer moving parts than one with its own content management system, so there is less to keep updated. What happens if something goes wrong? The role of backups

A backup is a saved copy of your website that can be restored if something breaks, and someone should be making them automatically. Without one, a mistake, a failed update or a hack can mean starting again from scratch.

It's not enough to know that backups exist. You want to know how often they're taken, where they're stored, how long they're kept and who restores them. A backup stored in the same place as the site is better than nothing, but it can disappear along with it.

The honest test is this: if your site vanished tomorrow, who would bring it back, and how long would it take? A good provider can answer that without hesitating.

Who makes edits to my website?

Day-to-day edits can be done by you, if the site is built to let you, or by your provider if you'd rather hand them over. Either is fine, as long as you've agreed which it is.

Typical edits include changing text, swapping photos, adding a new team member, updating prices or posting a news item. If you're comfortable with a simple editor, doing these yourself is quick and costs nothing. If it sounds like a chore, ask what happens when you send a request: how you send it, how fast it's done and whether there's a charge.

The most common frustration we hear about is the site that's technically yours but impossible to change without calling the person who built it. Ask for training at handover, and for the site to be built so you can manage it yourself where it makes sense.

What are monthly improvements, and do you need them?

Monthly improvements are small, steady changes based on how people actually use your site. They're not essential for every business, but they're how a website keeps earning its place rather than slowly going stale.

This might mean tidying up a page that people leave quickly, adding a section to answer a question customers keep asking, or sharpening your pages so they're easier to find in Google. It's also where reporting comes in. At Tova, we measure, improve and report back in plain language each month, because we think a site should keep getting better after it goes live.

Not everyone wants this, and that's fine. Some businesses just need the lights kept on. The point is to choose on purpose, instead of drifting. If you're unsure how your site is performing right now, our article on how to tell if your website is hurting your business is a good place to start.

What should you ask a provider before you sign?

Ask who does each job, how quickly they respond, and what you can take with you if you leave. Getting the answers in writing now saves a lot of awkwardness later.

Here are questions worth asking any web designer or agency about ongoing support:

  • Who hosts the site, and whose name is the hosting account in?
  • Whose name is the domain registered in?
  • Who does software updates and security checks, and how often?
  • Are backups automatic? Where are they kept and who restores them?
  • What happens if the site goes down or gets hacked, and who do I call?
  • How do small edits work, and are they included or charged separately?
  • How quickly will you reply when I ask for help?
  • What does the ongoing support cost, and what exactly does it cover?
  • Can I leave, and will you hand over the files, content and logins?
  • Will I get plain-language reports, or just an invoice?

Pay attention to how the answers feel as much as what they say. Clear, patient and specific is a good sign. Vague or defensive is worth noticing. Our guide to choosing a web designer in Christchurch covers more of what to look for.

So what should you do this week?

If you already have a site, find out who's looking after it today. If you're planning a new one, add ongoing care to your list of questions before you sign.

  1. Find out where your site is hosted and whose account it's in.
  2. Check who the domain name is registered to.
  3. Ask when your site was last updated and backed up.
  4. Decide which edits you'll do yourself and which you'll hand over.
  5. Put the answers in writing, even if it's just an email.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need website maintenance if my site is already built?

Yes. A website is never really finished, because the software it runs on keeps changing and new security problems keep turning up. Without regular care, a site slowly becomes slower, less secure and more likely to break.

What is the difference between website hosting and website maintenance?

Hosting is the service that keeps your website online and available to visitors. Maintenance is the ongoing work of updating, securing, backing up and fixing the site itself. Some providers bundle them together, and others treat them as separate jobs, so always ask.

Can I look after my own website?

You can often handle everyday edits yourself, such as changing text, photos or opening hours, if the site is built to let you. Security updates, backups and hosting are usually better left to someone with the technical know-how. What matters is that you know exactly which jobs are yours.

Can I move my website to a different provider later?

You should be able to. Ask before you sign who owns the website files, the domain name and the content, and how they would be handed over if you left. A good provider will answer plainly and put it in writing.
