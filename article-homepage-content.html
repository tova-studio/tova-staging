What should I put on my website's homepage?

By the Tova Digital team · 22 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Put five things on your homepage, in this order: a clear promise about what you do, who it's for, your main services, proof that you do good work, and one obvious next step. Everything else can live on other pages. If a visitor can't tell what you offer in a few seconds, they leave.

Most homepages try to say everything. The result is a pile of announcements, stock photos and vague words that leaves a visitor unsure whether they're in the right place.

A good homepage does something simpler. It answers the questions a stranger has in the order they ask them. It walks through those five sections, then gives you an outline to fill in and the mistakes that send people away.

What is a homepage actually for?

Your homepage has one job: to help the right person quickly decide that you're worth a closer look, and then show them how to take the next step. It's a front door, not a filing cabinet.

Many visitors won't arrive here first. They might land on a service page from Google, then click to your homepage to check you're a real business. Others type your name after a recommendation. Either way, they're asking the same things: what do you do, is it for me, can I trust you, and what do I do now?

Build the page around those four questions and most of the decisions get easier.

1. What is your clear promise?

The very top of your homepage should say, in plain words, what you do and who benefits. This is usually a headline plus one supporting sentence and a button.

Skip clever slogans that need decoding. "Good things, made with care" is a lovely sentiment, but a stranger still needs to know whether you fix roofs or write contracts. Say the useful thing first and keep your personality for the way you say it.

A simple formula works well: we help [who] get [what they want] by [what you do]. You'll rarely use it word for word, but it keeps you honest.

Add a photo that shows your real work, premises or team, rather than a generic handshake. Real beats polished stock almost every time.

2. Who is it for?

Next, tell visitors who you work with, so the right people feel recognised and the wrong ones can move on kindly. Naming your audience makes people feel understood.

This can be as short as a line or two. "We work with Canterbury homeowners planning a renovation" is far stronger than "we work with everyone". If you serve a particular area, mention it. Locals like to know you're nearby, and it helps search engines understand where you work.

If you serve more than one kind of customer, give each a short pathway, such as "For homeowners" and "For builders". Don't try to speak to all of them in one blurry paragraph.

3. How should you show your services?

Show your main services as a short, scannable list, with a few plain words about each and a link to learn more. Three to six is usually plenty, because too many choices make people freeze.

Describe services in terms of what the customer gets, not your internal jargon. "Fixing leaking taps and burst pipes" tells someone more than "plumbing solutions". Explain any technical term the first time it appears.

Keep the detail for each service's own page. Your homepage just needs to help people see that you do the thing they came for. If you'd like an example of the idea, our own home page sets out what we do in three short sections and links on from there.

4. What counts as proof?

Proof is anything that shows you do good work and can be trusted. It answers the quiet worry every visitor has: "Will they actually deliver?"

Good proof comes in several forms:

  • Real customer testimonials, ideally with a first name and the kind of work done.
  • Photos or examples of finished work, such as our case studies.
  • Logos of well-known customers or partners, if you have permission to show them.
  • Qualifications, memberships or accreditations that matter in your field.
  • A plain explanation of how you work, so there are no surprises.

Only share what's true and that you're allowed to share. Honest, modest proof is more convincing than big claims. If you're just starting out and don't have much yet, a clear process and a straightforward promise still do a lot of work.

5. What is the next step?

Finish with one obvious next step, such as "Call us", "Book a free chat" or "Get a quote". Visitors who are ready to act shouldn't have to hunt for how.

Pick the single action that suits your business best and repeat it at the top and bottom of the page. A button that says exactly what happens next ("Request a quote") beats a vague one ("Submit"). Make your phone number tap-to-call on phones, which our article on making your website easy to use on a phone explains.

You can offer a quieter second option for people who aren't ready, like a link to read more about how you work. But keep one main button clearly in front.

What is a fill-in-the-blanks homepage outline?

It's a simple template of the five sections above that you can complete in an afternoon. Write each answer in plain words, then trim.

  1. Headline: We help [who] with [what they need].
  2. Supporting line: [One sentence on how you do it, or where you work.]
  3. Main button: [The action you most want, such as "Get a quote".]
  4. Who it's for: You're in the right place if [describe your ideal customer and their situation].
  5. Services: [Three to six services, each with a plain one-line benefit and a link.]
  6. Proof: [One or two testimonials, a few examples of work, and any credentials.]
  7. How it works: [Three or four short steps from first contact to finished job.]
  8. Final invitation: [Repeat the main button, with your phone number and email nearby.]

Read it aloud when you're done. If it sounds like you talking to a customer, you're close. If it sounds like a brochure from 1998, simplify.

What mistakes make visitors leave your homepage?

Most visitors leave because they can't quickly tell what you do or what to do next. The same handful of mistakes crop up again and again:

  • A vague headline. Words like "innovative" and "passionate" say nothing about what you actually sell.
  • Talking about yourself first. A long company history above the fold, instead of what the customer gets.
  • Too many choices. A dozen buttons and menu items competing for attention.
  • Slideshows and auto-playing video. They move before people finish reading and can bury your main message.
  • Hidden contact details. If people have to dig for your phone number, many won't.
  • No proof. Claims without any evidence make wary visitors more wary.
  • Heavy, slow pages. Huge photos that crawl on mobile data.
  • Stale content. An out-of-date offer or last year's news makes a business look closed.

If several of those sound familiar, our article on how to tell if your website is hurting your business can help you decide what to fix first.

So what should you do this week?

Open your homepage on your phone and look only at the top of the screen. Can a stranger tell what you do, who it's for and what to do next? If not, start there.

Then work through the outline, one section at a time. Fix the headline, make the main button obvious, and add one real piece of proof. Small changes often make a big difference.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a homepage be?

Long enough to cover the five essentials and no longer. For most small businesses that is a handful of short sections that a visitor can scroll through comfortably on a phone. If a section doesn't help someone decide to get in touch, it probably belongs on another page.

Should my homepage have a slideshow?

Usually not. Slideshows move on before people finish reading, and they often hide your main message behind the second or third slide. One strong headline and one clear photo does the job better.

Do I need to put my prices on my homepage?

Not necessarily, but give visitors a sense of how you work and what to expect. If you can share starting prices or a typical range, say so on a services or pricing page and link to it. Being open about cost builds trust with people who are wary of surprises.

What if I don't have testimonials or past work to show yet?

Show other kinds of proof instead. That could be your qualifications, how long you have been doing the work, a clear explanation of your process, or a guarantee you stand behind. Then ask your first few customers if they'd be happy to say a few words.
