Can I update my own website without a developer?

By the Tova Digital team · 27 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Yes, usually. If your website is built on a content management system (CMS), you can edit text, swap photos, add pages and post news yourself, using screens that feel like a word processor. Design changes, new features, security and hosting are better left to a professional. Ask about handover and training before you commit.

Many business owners worry about the same thing: paying for a website and then having to ring someone, and wait, every time a phone number changes. It's a fair worry.

The good news is that it's something you can sort out before the site is built. This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. It explains what a content management system is, what's easy to do yourself, and what to ask a designer about handover.

What is a content management system?

A content management system, or CMS, is a private control panel behind your website that lets you change what's on it without writing any code. You log in, edit the words and photos in simple forms, and press publish.

Think of it like the difference between a printed poster and a noticeboard. A poster has to go back to the printer for every change. A noticeboard, you can update yourself in a minute.

Not every website has one. Some sites are built as fixed pages, a bit like an online business card. They can look lovely and load quickly, but changing them usually means asking the person who built them. That's a perfectly reasonable choice for a site that rarely changes, as long as you know that's the deal.

Which changes are easy to do yourself?

The everyday changes that keep a site fresh and accurate are the ones to take into your own hands. In a well-set-up CMS these are usually quick and low risk.

  • Editing words: opening hours, a new phone number, a team update, a fixed typo.
  • Swapping photos: a new shot of your shop, a finished job or a new team member.
  • Posting news or articles: a short update, a seasonal offer or a helpful how-to.
  • Adding or hiding a simple page: a new service, a new location or an event.
  • Updating prices or product details: where your site is set up for it.

These are the jobs that come up weekly or monthly. Being able to do them the same afternoon makes a real difference, especially when the information is time sensitive.

Which changes are better left to a professional?

Anything that changes how the site is built, rather than what it says, is best left to your designer or engineer. It's not that you couldn't try. It's that small mistakes here can ripple across the whole site.

  • Redesigning the layout: moving sections around, changing the look or restructuring the menu.
  • New features: booking systems, payment options, member logins or connections to other software.
  • Security and updates: keeping the software safe and current.
  • Hosting and domain settings: where the site lives and how your web address points to it.
  • Speed and technical search work: things that don't show on the page but affect how it performs.

A good build makes this division clear. You get a safe space to change the content, and the structural parts stay protected. That's often a feature, not a limitation. It means you can't accidentally break the design while fixing a typo.

Will I be locked out of my own website?

You shouldn't be, but it's worth checking, because the answer depends on what you agree at the start. Being locked out usually comes down to unclear ownership, not to the technology.

Before you sign anything, find out who owns and controls each of these:

  • Your domain name (your web address).
  • Your hosting account, where the site lives.
  • The content: your words, photos and logo.
  • The admin login to your CMS, with you as the main user.
  • The design files and the site's code, and whether you can take them elsewhere.

Get the answers in writing, in plain language. If a designer is vague or uncomfortable about it, treat that as useful information. Our guide to choosing a web designer in Christchurch has more on the questions worth asking.

What should you ask about handover and training?

Ask exactly what you'll be handed on launch day and how you'll be shown to use it. A website you can't confidently edit is no better than one without a CMS.

Here are the questions we'd want answered if we were in your shoes:

  1. Will there be a CMS, and which one? Ask what it is, and whether it suits the way you'll actually work.
  2. Will I get training? Ideally a live walk-through using your own site, not just a link to a manual.
  3. Is there written or recorded help? Something you can come back to in six months when you've forgotten the steps.
  4. What can I change, and what can't I? A clear list saves guessing.
  5. How do I undo a mistake? And are backups kept?
  6. Who do I contact when I'm stuck? And how quickly can I expect a reply?
  7. What happens if I part ways with you? Can I take my site, my content and my logins with me?

Is a CMS always the right choice?

No. A CMS is worth having when you expect to change the site regularly, and it's unnecessary when you won't. Choosing well comes down to being honest about how often you'll really log in.

If you'll post news, add services or update products often, a CMS gives you independence and saves waiting. If your site is a few pages that might change once a year, a simpler build can be cheaper and sturdier, and it's fine to ask your designer to make those occasional changes.

Cost is part of this decision too, since a site with a CMS is a bigger build. Our article on what a small business website costs in NZ explains what affects the price.

So what should you do next?

Start by listing the changes you expect to make in the next year, and who you'd like to make them. That list tells you whether you need a CMS and how much training you'll want.

Frequently asked questions

What is a content management system?

A content management system, or CMS, is a private control panel for your website. You log in, change the words and photos in simple forms, and press publish. You don't need to touch any code.

Can I break my website by editing it myself?

It's hard to do serious damage when you're only editing text and photos in a well-built CMS. The usual slips, like a typo or an oversized photo, are easy to fix. Ask your designer how to undo a change and whether backups are kept.

Do I own my website if a designer built it?

You should, but it depends on what you agree. Ask in writing who owns the domain name, the content, the design files and the login details. A good designer will answer plainly and put the answer in the quote or agreement.

Do I need a CMS if I rarely change my website?

Not always. A simple site that rarely changes, like an online business card, can work well without one. If you expect to add news, products or new pages regularly, a CMS will save you time and waiting.
