How do I make my website easy to use on a phone?

By the Tova Digital team · 20 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Test your site on your own phone, then fix what frustrates you. Make buttons big enough to tap, text readable without zooming, forms short, your phone number one tap to call, and pages quick to load. That's what good responsive design delivers, and it's what to ask your designer for.

Most visitors will meet your business on a phone first. If the site is fiddly there, they won't pinch, zoom and persevere. They'll go back to the search results and try the next business.

The good news is that you can check this yourself, with no tools and no technical knowledge. This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. It gives you a simple phone test, then the five things that matter most.

What makes a website mobile friendly?

A mobile friendly website is one that's comfortable to read and use on a small touchscreen without any pinching, zooming or sideways scrolling. The usual way to build one is called responsive design.

Responsive design just means the site rearranges itself to fit the screen. On a computer you might see three columns. On a phone those columns stack into one, the menu folds away and the text stays a comfortable size.

The catch is that "responsive" is easy to claim and harder to do well. A site can technically shrink to fit a phone and still be miserable to use. That's why testing it with your own thumbs matters.

How do you test your website on your phone?

Pick up your phone, open your site as a stranger would and try to do the things a customer wants to do. Five minutes is enough to spot most problems.

Here's a simple routine:

  1. Use mobile data, not wifi. Customers are often on a patchy connection, in the car park or on the couch.
  2. Start from Google. Search for your business or service and tap through to your site, rather than typing the address.
  3. Hold the phone in one hand. Can your thumb reach the menu and the main buttons?
  4. Try to call, book or enquire. Do the main thing a customer would want, and notice every moment of friction.
  5. Read a full page without zooming. If you want to pinch to read, so will your customers.
  6. Turn the phone sideways. Check nothing breaks.
  7. Ask someone else. A friend or family member who has never seen the site will find things you've stopped noticing.

Write down every moment where you hesitated, mis-tapped or sighed. That list is a ready-made brief for a designer. If it's long, our article on how to tell if your website is hurting your business can help you work out what to fix first.

Are your buttons and links easy to tap?

Buttons and links should be about the size of a fingertip, with enough space around them that you never hit the wrong one. A finger is far less precise than a mouse pointer.

Watch for these problems when you test:

  • Links in a row of text that sit so close together you tap the wrong one.
  • Tiny menu icons or close buttons that take two or three tries.
  • Buttons that look fine on a desktop but shrink to a sliver on a phone.
  • Important buttons tucked into the top corner, where a thumb can't easily reach.

The fix is usually simple: bigger tap areas, more space between them, and one clear main button on each page. Ask your designer to check every page, not just the home page.

Is your text readable without zooming?

Text should be readable at arm's length without zooming, with good contrast against the background. If you squint, your customers are squinting too.

The usual culprits are small body text, thin grey type on a white background and long, dense paragraphs that turn into a wall of words on a narrow screen. Try reading your site outdoors in daylight, because glare shows up weak contrast quickly.

Short paragraphs, clear headings and plenty of white space all help. So does putting the most important information, such as what you do and where, near the top.

Are your forms short enough for a phone?

Keep forms as short as you can, because typing on a phone is slow and every extra field loses someone. Ask only for what you genuinely need to reply.

For most small businesses that means a name, a way to contact the person and a short message. Anything else can wait until you've started talking.

Also check the small things:

  • The email field brings up the email keyboard, and the phone field brings up the number pad.
  • Each field has a clear label that stays visible while you type.
  • Error messages say plainly what to fix.
  • The submit button is easy to find and tap, and you get a clear thank-you afterwards.

Then actually send a test enquiry from your phone and see that it arrives. It's a surprisingly common thing to find broken.

Can customers call you with one tap?

Yes, and they should be able to. Your phone number should be a tap-to-call link, so tapping it opens the phone's dialler instead of making people copy and paste digits.

It's a small technical detail, often called click-to-call, and it makes a big difference for businesses that rely on calls, such as trades, clinics and local services. Put it somewhere obvious, like the top of every page or a button that stays in view as people scroll.

Test it yourself. Tap your number on your phone and see what happens. If nothing does, or you have to select the text first, ask your designer to turn it into a proper link. The same goes for your email address and a map link for your address.

Does your website load quickly on a phone?

Your site should show something useful almost straight away, even on a mediocre connection. People are impatient on phones, and a slow page often loses them before they've read a word.

You don't need to be technical to notice this. When you test on mobile data, notice how long you wait before you can read the page and tap something. If you find yourself waiting, that's the signal.

The most common cause is large, uncompressed photos. Others include too many add-ons and plugins, heavy animations and cheap hosting. These are all fixable. A designer and engineer working together can usually sort the lot, and a free tool like Google PageSpeed Insights can point to the biggest culprits.

How do you brief your designer about mobile?

Share your test notes, then ask your designer to design for the phone first and check the result on real devices. This keeps mobile from being an afterthought squeezed in at the end.

These questions are worth asking any designer:

  • Will you design the phone version first, or the desktop version?
  • How will you test the site on real phones, not just in a browser window?
  • Will my phone number be a tap-to-call link on every page?
  • How will you keep the photos and pages quick to load?
  • Can I try the site on my own phone before it goes live?

If you're planning a bigger overhaul, our website redesign checklist covers what to have ready. You can also look at the sites in our case studies on your phone and judge for yourself.

So what should you do this week?

Run the phone test today, write down what annoyed you, and fix the quick wins first. Usually that means the phone number, the main button and the form.

Then decide whether the rest can be adjusted or whether the site needs a proper rethink. Either way, you'll now be able to talk about it in plain terms. Our FAQ page answers other common questions about working with a studio.

Frequently asked questions

What does responsive design mean?

Responsive design means a website rearranges itself to suit the screen it's on, whether that's a phone, a tablet or a desktop computer. The same site adapts instead of you needing separate versions. It's the standard way to build a mobile friendly website today.

Do I need a separate mobile website?

Almost never. One responsive site that adapts to every screen is easier to keep up to date and easier for people to share and for Google to understand. Two separate sites usually means double the maintenance.

Why does my website look fine on my computer but wrong on my phone?

The site was probably designed for a wide screen first and never properly adapted for a narrow one. Common signs are tiny text, buttons squeezed together and content that spills off the side. A designer can usually fix this, though sometimes an older site needs rebuilding.

How can I check how fast my website is on mobile?

Open your site on your phone using mobile data rather than wifi, and notice how long it takes before you can read and tap things. For a more detailed report, free tools such as Google PageSpeed Insights will test any page address. Share the results with your designer if anything is flagged.
