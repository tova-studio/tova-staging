How do I know if my website is secure?

By the Tova Digital team · 26 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

You can check the basics in ten minutes. Look for the padlock and https in your address bar, confirm your website software and plugins are up to date, use strong unique passwords with two-factor login, and make sure recent backups exist and can be restored. If anything is unclear or looks wrong, ask a professional.

Most business owners worry about being hacked, but few know what to actually look at. It can feel like a job only a technical person could do.

It isn't. The most common problems are surprisingly ordinary, and you can spot many of them yourself. This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. We'll walk through the five areas that matter most, then give you a checklist to run in ten minutes.

What does "a secure website" actually mean?

A secure website is one where visitors' information travels privately, the software is kept current, only the right people can log in, and you can recover quickly if something goes wrong. That's it. No single trick covers all four.

Think of it like your shop. A good lock matters, but so does knowing who has a key, fixing the broken window and having insurance. Website security works the same way, in layers.

Does your website use HTTPS?

HTTPS means the connection between your visitor and your website is private and scrambled, so nobody can read it on the way past. You can see it in the address bar: the web address starts with https and there's a small padlock.

You'll also hear it called SSL, which is the older name for the same idea. In NZ searches, "https ssl" usually means exactly this. The padlock matters most if your site has a contact form, a booking form or a login, because those send personal details.

Check every page, not just the home page. Also type your address starting with http instead of https and see whether it jumps across to the secure version. If your browser shows a "Not secure" warning, visitors will see it too, and many will leave.

Ask your hosting company or web designer to fix this. It's usually quick, and it's not something to put off.

Is your website software up to date?

Out-of-date software is one of the most common ways websites get broken into. Website platforms, themes and plugins (the add-ons that give a site extra features) all release updates, and many of those updates close known security holes.

Attackers often use automated tools that hunt for sites running old versions. They aren't targeting you personally, they're simply working through a very long list.

If you can log in to your site's admin area, look for an updates notice. If updates have been waiting for months, that's a warning sign. Also ask yourself whether anyone is actually responsible for doing them. On many small business sites, the honest answer is "nobody", and that's the real problem.

Plugins deserve a special look. Remove any you don't use, because every unused add-on is one more door left unlocked.

Are your passwords and logins strong enough?

Strong, unique passwords plus two-factor login are the best protection you can give your site for the least effort. Most break-ins start with a password that was weak, shared or reused from somewhere else.

Here's what good looks like:

  • Long and unique. A passphrase of several unrelated words is easier to remember than a jumble of symbols, and harder to guess.
  • Never reused. If one site you use gets breached, reused passwords let attackers try the same one on yours.
  • In a password manager. It remembers everything for you, so you don't need to write passwords on sticky notes.
  • Two-factor login switched on. This asks for a second proof, usually a code on your phone, as well as your password.
  • Only the right people have access. Remove accounts for former staff, old contractors and anyone who no longer needs one.

Two-factor login is worth turning on for your website admin, your hosting account, your domain name account and your business email. Email matters more than most people realise, because anyone who controls it can reset the rest.

Do you have backups, and do they work?

A backup is a saved copy of your website that lets you restore it if it's damaged, hacked or deleted by mistake. It's your safety net, and it only counts if it's recent and you know it can be restored.

Many owners assume their hosting company handles this. Sometimes it does, and sometimes the backups are limited, out of date or stored in the same place as the site. Ask directly: how often are backups made, where are they kept, and how long would a restore take?

A backup that has never been tested is a hope, not a plan. Ask someone to confirm that a restore actually works, ideally before you need it.

What is the ten-minute website security checklist?

Work through these steps with your phone and a cup of tea. Each one is a yes or no question, and any "no" or "I don't know" is worth following up.

  1. Padlock check. Does your home page, contact page and any form page show https and a padlock?
  2. Redirect check. Does typing http at the start of your address jump to the https version?
  3. Warning check. Does your browser ever say "Not secure" or show a warning about your site?
  4. Update check. Are the platform, theme and plugins up to date, and do you know who updates them?
  5. Plugin check. Have you removed add-ons you no longer use?
  6. Password check. Is every login to your site, hosting and domain strong, unique and stored safely?
  7. Two-factor check. Is two-factor login switched on for your site admin, hosting, domain and email?
  8. Access check. Can anyone log in who shouldn't, such as a former employee or contractor?
  9. Backup check. Do you know how often backups run, where they live and who can restore them?
  10. Strange-behaviour check. Search for your business on Google. Do any results show odd titles or unfamiliar pages, or does the site redirect somewhere unexpected?

If you can say yes to most of these, you're in better shape than a lot of small business sites. If you're not sure about several, don't panic. It simply tells you where to start. When should you call a professional?

Call someone as soon as you see signs of a break-in, or whenever you can't answer the checklist questions with confidence. Security is one area where it pays to ask early.

These are good reasons to get help:

  • Your site shows pages, links or ads you didn't put there.
  • Visitors or Google warn that your site may be unsafe.
  • You can't log in, or your password was changed without you.
  • You take payments, bookings or customer details through the site.
  • Nobody knows who is looking after updates and backups.
  • Your site is old and was built by someone you can no longer reach.

A professional can do the checks you can't, such as reviewing how the site is built and set up. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between HTTPS and SSL?

SSL is the older name for the technology that scrambles data between your visitor and your website. The modern version is technically called TLS, but most people still say SSL. HTTPS is simply the web address starting with https, which shows that this protection is switched on.

Is a padlock enough to prove my website is safe?

No. The padlock only tells you the connection is private. It says nothing about whether your software is up to date, your passwords are strong or your backups exist. Treat it as the first check, not the only one.

Do small businesses really get hacked?

Yes. Most attacks are automated and look for common weaknesses such as out-of-date software and reused passwords, rather than picking a particular business. Being small does not make a site invisible, which is why the basics matter for everyone.

What should I do if I think my website has been hacked?

Change your passwords for the website, hosting and email, and contact whoever looks after your site straight away. Do not delete anything until someone has had a look, as a clean backup and the site's records help with recovery. Let your customers know if their details may have been affected.
