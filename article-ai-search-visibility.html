How do you get your business mentioned by ChatGPT?

By the Tova Digital team · 19 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

AI assistants mention businesses whose websites answer questions clearly, state the same details everywhere, and are backed by reviews and mentions elsewhere. Nobody can guarantee a mention, but you can make your site easier to quote: answer-first writing, structured data, consistent business details, an FAQ and a clear page about who you are.

More and more people are skipping the list of blue links and simply asking an AI assistant, "Who's a good electrician in Christchurch?" or "How do I choose an accountant?" If your business is named in the answer, that's a lovely thing. If it isn't, it's fair to wonder why.

This article explains, in plain words, how AI assistants tend to pick the sources they use, and what you can change on your website to make yours easier to quote. It's written by Tova Digital, a small web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch. We'll also be honest about what nobody, including us, can promise.

How do AI assistants decide which businesses to mention?

They tend to favour sources that are clear, consistent and backed up by other people. The exact workings are private and change often, so be a little wary of anyone who claims to know the recipe.

What we can say is that assistants build their answers from text they can read and understand. That includes your website, other websites that mention you, and sometimes live search results. A source that states things plainly is easier to use than one that makes the reader guess.

Think of it like a friend being asked for a recommendation. They'll suggest the business they understand, know the details of, and have heard good things about. Your job is to make all three easy.

Why do clear answers matter most?

Because an assistant needs a sentence it can lift out and trust. If your page buries the answer under a long, wandering introduction, there is nothing tidy to quote.

Say what you do, who it's for and where, near the top of the page. Put the answer to a question straight after the heading that asks it. Then explain.

This is also better for your human readers, who are busy and would like the point first. Writing for people and writing for assistants turn out to be nearly the same job.

What is structured data, and do you need it?

Structured data is a small piece of hidden code on your page that labels information in a way machines can read without guessing. It tells a search engine, "this is the business name, this is the address, this is a question and its answer."

You can't see it on the page, but it sits behind the scenes. Common labels include your business details, your opening hours, your articles and your frequently asked questions. It's not magic, and it doesn't guarantee anything. It simply removes guesswork, which is a good thing to remove.

This is the kind of job where having design and engineering under one roof helps, because it needs to be written carefully and kept in step with what the page actually says. It must always match the visible text, otherwise it does more harm than good.

Why do consistent business details matter?

Because assistants and search engines build confidence by finding the same facts in several places. If your name, address, phone number and services read differently on your website, your Google Business Profile and your social pages, you create doubt.

It's a dull job, but it's worth doing once and doing properly. Check these things match everywhere:

  • Your business name, written the same way each time.
  • Your address and the areas you serve, such as Christchurch and wider Canterbury.
  • Your phone number and email address.
  • Your opening hours, including public holidays.
  • A short description of what you do, in similar words.

Old directory listings are the usual culprits. A former address or a retired phone number can hang around for years.

Do reviews and mentions elsewhere help?

Yes, because they are the part you can't write yourself. Anyone can say they're great on their own website. When other people say it, it carries more weight.

Genuine reviews on Google, mentions in local news or industry sites, and listings in respectable directories all show that your business is real and that people have dealt with it. Ask happy customers for an honest review, and reply to the ones you get, kindly and briefly.

Please don't buy reviews or invent them. It's against the platforms' rules, it's unkind to your customers, and it's the opposite of the honest reputation you're trying to build.

What are five on-page changes that make your site easier to quote?

Start with these five, which all sit on your own website and are within your control:

  • Lead with the answer. Open each page and each section with a direct sentence that answers the question it raises, then explain.
  • Use headings that are real questions. Write them the way a customer would ask them, for example "How long does a roof repair take?" rather than "Our process".
  • Add a proper FAQ. Answer the follow-up questions people actually ask you, in two to four plain sentences each, and mark them up with structured data.
  • Make your business details unmistakable. Put your name, location, services and contact details in clear text on your contact page and footer, and keep them identical elsewhere.
  • Show who is behind the site. Have an about page that says who you are, what you do and where you're based, and put a clear author on your articles.

None of these needs a big rebuild. Many can be done page by page over a few weeks. You can see how we handle the structure side in our marketing and growth work, and our FAQ page shows the question-and-answer style in action.

What can nobody promise you?

Nobody can promise that ChatGPT or any other assistant will mention you. The assistants decide for themselves, the rules change without notice, and your competitors are working on this too.

If someone guarantees you'll be recommended, or sells you a secret trick, treat it the way you'd treat a promise of a number one spot on Google. What an honest person can offer is a list of sensible changes, a clear explanation of why each one helps, and a plain-spoken way to check how things are going.

It also helps to remember that these changes pay off even if no assistant ever names you. A faster, clearer, more trustworthy website serves every visitor, and that's rarely wasted effort.

So what should you do this month?

Keep it small and steady, and work through it in this order:

  • Search for your own business and note what comes up and whether the details are right.
  • Fix any mismatched names, addresses or phone numbers across your listings and social pages.
  • Rewrite the top of your most important pages so each opens with a clear answer.
  • Add or improve an FAQ on the pages where customers ask the most questions.
  • Ask a few recent customers for an honest review.

Frequently asked questions

Can I pay to be recommended by ChatGPT?

There is no simple way to buy a place in an AI assistant's answers, and be wary of anyone who says there is. What you can do is make your website clear, consistent and easy to quote, and build up genuine reviews and mentions. Those are the things that give an assistant a reason to include you.

Is GEO different from SEO?

GEO, short for generative engine optimisation, is about being included in AI-written answers, while SEO is about ranking in a list of search results. In practice they overlap a great deal. A clear, trustworthy, well-built website helps with both.

How long does it take to show up in AI search?

Nobody can give you an honest date, because it depends on the assistant, your field and how much competition you have. Changes to your site can be made quickly, but it takes time for them to be noticed and trusted. Treat it as steady work rather than a switch you flick.

Do small local businesses have a chance of being mentioned?

Yes. People often ask assistants for local recommendations, and a small business with clear details, genuine reviews and a helpful website can be a good match for those questions. You don't need to be big, you need to be easy to understand and easy to trust.
