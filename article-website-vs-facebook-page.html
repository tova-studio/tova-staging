Do I need a website if I already have a Facebook page?

By the Tova Digital team · 21 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Often yes. A Facebook page is useful, but you rent it on terms Meta can change. A website is yours: it shows up in Google, builds trust, keeps your information in one place and turns visitors into enquiries. A page alone can be enough for some businesses; the test below shows if yours is one.

Plenty of Kiwi small businesses run on a Facebook page alone, and it's easy to see why. It's free, it's quick to set up and your customers are already there.

The question is whether that's enough, or whether you're building your business on someone else's land. This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. We'll compare the two honestly and finish with a simple test you can run yourself.

What's the real difference between a website and a Facebook page?

A website is something you own, and a Facebook page is something you rent. That one idea explains almost everything else in this article.

With a website, you choose the address, the layout, the words and what happens when someone gets in touch. With a Facebook page, you're a tenant in Meta's building. You get a template, a feed and whatever features Meta decides to offer this year.

Renting isn't wrong. Plenty of good businesses rent shop space. But you'd want to know the landlord can change the rules, and that you can't take the shopfront with you if you leave.

Will customers find you on Google with only a Facebook page?

Sometimes, but not reliably. Your page may appear when someone searches your exact business name, yet it's much less likely to appear when they search for what you do.

Think about how a stranger looks for you. They type something like "plumber Christchurch" or "cafe near Sumner" and scan the results. Google is trying to show pages that clearly answer that search, and a website can have a dedicated page for each service and each place you work.

A Facebook page is mostly one page with a feed of posts, and much of its content sits behind a login or a scroll. If you'd like to understand how local search works, our article on how to show up in Google in Christchurch goes through it step by step.

There's a newer side to this too. People now ask AI assistants for recommendations, and those tools like clear, readable pages about who you are and what you do. We cover that in how to get your business mentioned by ChatGPT.

Does a website make people trust you more?

For many customers, yes. A website tells them you're established, easy to check out and happy to be found properly.

That matters most when the job is big, the cost is high or the customer has never heard of you. They want to see who you are, what you've done, how you work and how to reach you, all without scrolling back through years of posts.

A Facebook page can show some of that, but the story is scattered. A website lets you present your best work, your process and your answers to common questions in an order you choose.

Who's really in control of your Facebook page?

Meta is. You can post what you like, but the platform decides who sees it, how it looks and what you can do with it.

That control shows up in a few ways:

  • Reach. Who sees your posts depends on an algorithm you can't see or change.
  • Rules. Features, layouts and policies change, and you adapt or lose out.
  • Access. Accounts can be hacked, locked or closed, sometimes with little explanation.
  • Your audience. Followers belong to the platform's system, not to you. You can't take them elsewhere.

Links are a good recent example. Kiwi marketing coverage has reported that Meta has started charging businesses a monthly subscription to put links to their own website on their own Facebook page. Details may change, so check Meta's own information for how it applies to you.

Whatever the final detail, the lesson is the same. When you rent attention, the landlord can change what it costs.

Which one brings in more enquiries?

A website is built to turn a visitor into an enquiry, while a Facebook page is built to keep people on Facebook. Those are different jobs.

On your own site, you decide what the main button says, where the phone number sits and how short the contact form is. Nothing competes for attention. Nobody gets pulled away by a video, an advert or a friend's holiday photos.

On Facebook, an enquiry usually means a message, which is fine for some businesses. But links matter too. A website gives every service, every place and every story its own address that you can share, print on a van or put on a card. It's a stable home that all your other channels can point to.

When is a Facebook page enough? A simple test

A Facebook page is probably enough if you can answer yes to every question below. If you answer no to even one or two, a website is worth considering.

  1. Do nearly all your customers come from word of mouth or local groups? If strangers rarely search for you, search visibility matters less.
  2. Is what you sell simple? If people don't need to compare, read details or check credentials, a page can cope.
  3. Are you happy for customers to reach you only by message? No bookings, forms, payments or quotes needed.
  4. Could you cope if the page vanished tomorrow? Think about your customer list, your photos and your reputation.
  5. Are you comfortable with the platform's rules changing? That includes reach and, as above, what links might cost.

If you said no to the second, third or fourth, that's usually where a website starts earning its keep. A business that sells something people research, or where trust matters, tends to need one sooner.

So what should you do next?

Keep your Facebook page, and give it a proper home base to point to. You don't have to choose one or the other.

Here's a practical way to start:

  • Run the five-question test above and write down your honest answers.
  • Search for what you do in your area, the way a stranger would, and see who turns up.
  • Export or save your best photos, reviews and customer details somewhere you own.
  • Decide what you'd want a visitor to do first: call, book, ask for a quote or buy.
  • Ask for a plain-language quote. Our article on what a small business website costs in NZ explains what affects the price.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Facebook business page the same as a website?

No. A Facebook page lives inside Facebook, on Meta's rules and layout. A website sits at your own address, and you decide how it looks, what it says and how people contact you.

Can I run my business with only a Facebook page?

Some businesses do, especially those that work entirely by word of mouth and local community groups. The risk is that everything depends on one platform you don't control. If your customers find you through Google, a page alone will usually leave gaps.

Will a website help me show up on Google?

It gives you something Google can properly read, with a page for each service and place you work in. That doesn't guarantee a top spot, but it gives you a real chance. A Facebook page rarely competes well for the searches customers type.

Do I have to stop using Facebook if I get a website?

Not at all. The two work best together. Use Facebook to keep in touch and share news, and use your website as the solid home base that everything points back to.
