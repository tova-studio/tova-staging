How do you choose a web designer in Christchurch?

By the Tova Digital team · 18 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Choose a web designer by asking five plain questions: who will actually do the work, what the quote includes, who owns your site, how customers will find it, and who looks after it once it's live. Pick the one who answers clearly, without jargon, and makes you feel heard.

Choosing who builds your website is a bit like choosing a builder. You are handing over something important, and most of us can't judge the work just by looking at it. So the most useful thing you can do is know what to ask.

This article gives you the questions we'd ask if we were in your shoes. It's written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch, so we'll be upfront that we have a stake in the answer. We've tried to keep it fair to every kind of web designer, including the ones who aren't us.

Who will actually do the work?

The person you meet in the first conversation is not always the person who builds your website, so ask directly. Different kinds of providers work in different ways, and none of them is automatically wrong.

A larger agency often splits the job between several people, such as a salesperson, a project manager, a designer and a developer. A freelancer usually does everything alone. A small studio sits somewhere in between.

What matters is that you know who you are dealing with. Ask who you will talk to when you want a change, and who will be doing the design and the building. At Tova, design and engineering sit under one roof, and you speak directly to the people doing the work.

What should a quote include?

A good quote says, in plain words, what you will get, what you won't, and what happens if the plan changes. If you can't read a quote and picture the finished website, it needs more detail.

Here is a simple checklist to hold any quote up against:

  • The pages and features you are paying for, listed clearly.
  • How much design work is included, and how many rounds of feedback you get.
  • Who writes the words and finds the photos, you or them.
  • Whether hosting, your domain name, security and updates are included or extra.
  • Whether you will be shown how to edit the site yourself.
  • What counts as extra, and how it will be priced before any work starts.

When you compare quotes, compare them line by line. Two quotes that look different are often covering different things.

Who owns your website and everything on it?

You should. It's worth asking this in writing before any work begins, because it's much harder to sort out afterwards.

Your domain name (your web address) should be registered in your name or your business name. You should hold the logins for it and for your hosting, which is the service that keeps your site online. You should also be free to take your site elsewhere if the relationship ever stops working.

Most designers are completely above board about this. But a few set things up so that only they can make changes, and that can leave you stuck. A designer who is happy to explain exactly who holds what is usually one you can trust.

Will your website help customers find you?

A beautiful site that nobody finds is a brochure in a drawer. Ask how the designer will help people discover you, not just how the site will look.

Sensible answers include building it so it works well on phones, keeping it fast, writing clear page titles and descriptions, and making sure local customers searching for a business like yours in Christchurch or Canterbury can find you. If you serve a local area, ask about your Google Business Profile too, since it's often the first thing people see.

Be wary of anyone who guarantees you'll be number one on Google. Nobody controls that, and an honest designer will say so. Instead, listen for someone who explains what they will do and how you will know if it's working.

What happens after the website goes live?

Launch day is the start of a website's life, not the end of it. Software needs updating, security needs watching, and your business will change, so ask who looks after the site afterwards.

Find out who you contact when something breaks or when you want to change a page. Ask how quickly they usually reply, and whether ongoing care is part of the price or a separate arrangement. Then ask for that in writing too.

This is the part that people most often forget to ask about. It's also the part that decides whether you feel supported a year from now. It is one reason we put "Looked after" among the values we work by.

How can you tell if a web designer is a good fit?

Pay attention to how the first conversations feel, because that's a preview of the whole project. You are looking for someone who is curious about your business before they start talking about themselves.

Signs of a good fit tend to look like this:

  • They ask about your customers and what you want the site to achieve before they mention templates or technology.
  • They explain things in plain language and don't mind being asked twice.
  • They are comfortable saying what they won't do, or when something isn't right for you.
  • They show you past work and are happy for you to ask how it went.
  • You come away feeling heard, not sold to.

It's also fine to trust your instincts here. You'll be working with this person for weeks or months, so it's worth enjoying the conversation.

Is a small studio right for you?

A small studio suits many businesses, but not all of them, and it helps to know the trade-offs. The big advantage is directness: you talk to the people designing and building your site, with nobody passing messages along.

If you want to see how that works in practice, our services cover design, development and marketing under one roof, and our about page explains who we are and what we value. If it turns out we're not the right fit, we'll say so.

So what should you do?

Treat this as a short, calm process rather than a big decision made in a hurry. Here is a simple way to go about it:

  • Write down what you want your website to do for your business, in a few sentences.
  • Talk to two or three web designers and ask each of them the same questions.
  • Ask for a written quote that covers what is included, who owns what, and what happens after launch.
  • Compare the quotes line by line, not just by the bottom figure.
  • Choose the person you understood best and felt most heard by.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a website cost in Christchurch?

It depends on the size of the site, the features it needs and how much design, writing and photography is involved, so an honest figure starts with a conversation. Ask for a written quote that lists what is included, and compare quotes like for like. Be a little wary of any price given before someone has asked about your business.

Do I need to choose a web designer who is based in Christchurch?

No, but it can help. A local designer can meet you in person and understands how Canterbury customers look for businesses like yours. What matters more is that they listen, explain things plainly and are easy to reach.

Do I need to understand the technical side before I hire someone?

No. A good web designer explains things in plain words and asks about your customers before talking about technology. If an answer is full of jargon, ask them to put it another way. How they respond tells you a lot.

Who should own my domain name and website?

You should. Your domain name should be registered in your name or your business name, and you should hold the logins for it and for your hosting. That way you can move your site or change who looks after it whenever you choose.
