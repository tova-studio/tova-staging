How do I know if my website is hurting my business?

By the Tova Digital team · 19 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Your website may be hurting your business if it loads slowly, is awkward on a phone, leaves visitors unsure what to do next, looks out of date, or brings in few or no enquiries. A 15-minute check using your own phone and free tools will show you which of these apply.

Most business owners don't look at their own website the way a stranger does. You know where everything is, you know what you meant, and you've seen it a hundred times. A new visitor has none of that, and about a few seconds of patience.

This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. We started Tova because too many good businesses were being let down by websites that looked nice but didn't work. Here are the warning signs we'd look for first, and a quick way to check them yourself.

What are the signs a website is hurting your business?

The five most common signs are slow loading, poor use on a phone, an unclear next step, an outdated look, and a lack of enquiries. Any one of them can quietly send customers elsewhere.

The tricky part is that nobody tells you. A visitor who gives up doesn't ring to explain why. They simply go back to the search results and click the next business. So the damage is invisible unless you go looking for it.

None of these signs means you've done something wrong. Websites age, habits change and phones get better. It just means it's time for a calm, honest look.

Is your website too slow?

If your pages take a noticeable moment to appear, especially on a phone, you are probably losing people before they've read a word. Slow sites are frustrating, and frustrated people leave.

Common causes are very large photos, too many add-ons and cheap or crowded hosting. These are usually fixable without rebuilding anything. Compressing images alone can make a surprising difference.

You don't need to guess. Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool lets you paste in your address and shows how your page performs, with plain suggestions for improving it. Don't worry about every technical line. Look for the biggest problems it flags.

Does your website work properly on a phone?

If you have to pinch, zoom or scroll sideways to read your own site on a phone, it is working against you. Many people in Christchurch and around New Zealand will find you on their phone first, often while standing in a queue or on a couch.

Pick up your phone right now and try to do what a customer would do. Can you read the text without zooming? Are the buttons big enough to tap without hitting the wrong one? Can you find your phone number and tap it to call? Does a menu or pop-up get in the way?

Try it on mobile data as well as Wi-Fi, because that's closer to real life.

Is it obvious what to do next?

A good page makes the next step obvious, whether that's calling, booking, requesting a quote or buying. If a visitor has to hunt for it, many won't bother.

A simple test: show your home page to a friend for five seconds, then hide it and ask what you do and what they'd do next. If they can't say, that's useful information, and it's not their fault.

Warning signs include a contact page buried in a menu, five competing buttons on one screen, or a page that talks about you for a long time before it says how to get in touch. One clear action, repeated in sensible places, usually works better than many choices.

Does your website look out of date?

An outdated look matters because people judge trust quickly, and a tired-looking site can make a good business seem less careful than it really is. It's not about following fashion. It's about whether the site still looks like you.

Signs include old photos, a copyright year that stopped a while ago, services you no longer offer, broken links, and text that no longer matches how you talk to customers today. Small things like these add up.

Often a refresh of photos, wording and layout does more than a complete rebuild. Our case studies show how we approach a redesign when one is needed.

Are you getting enquiries?

If your website brings in few or no enquiries, that's the clearest sign it isn't working as hard as it should. It's also the hardest one to read, because there are several possible reasons.

People may not be finding you at all, which is a visibility question. They may be finding you and leaving, which points to speed, phone use or an unclear next step. Or your contact form might be broken, which happens more often than you'd think.

Test your own form and phone number first. Then think about where your enquiries do come from, such as referrals, social media or Google, and whether your website is helping or getting in the way. What is the 15-minute self-audit?

It's a quick check you can do with your own phone, a laptop and free tools, and it covers each warning sign above. Set a timer and don't fix anything yet. Just write down what you notice.

  • Search for your business. Type your business name and what you do into Google. Check that your site appears, and that the details shown are correct.
  • Run PageSpeed Insights. Paste in your home page address and note the main problems it lists, especially anything about images or loading time.
  • Use your phone. Open your site on mobile data. Try to read it, tap the menu, and call or message from the page.
  • Do the five-second test. Ask someone who doesn't know your business to look at your home page and tell you what you do and what to do next.
  • Test your contact routes. Send yourself a message through the contact form and check that it arrives. Call the number on the site.
  • Read it with fresh eyes. Look for old photos, outdated services, broken links and a copyright year that's behind.

When the timer rings, circle the one or two problems that would most likely cost you a customer. Those come first.

What should you do with what you find?

Start with the biggest obstacle between a visitor and an enquiry, and fix that before anything else. Small, steady changes usually beat a big overhaul done in a panic.

Many fixes are simple: smaller images, a clearer button, updated wording, a working form. Others, such as a site that can't be edited or can't cope with phones, may need a more serious conversation. Frequently asked questions

How often should I check my website?

A quick look every few months is plenty for most small businesses, and again after any change. Do it more often when you launch a promotion or notice enquiries dropping. Put a reminder in your calendar so it doesn't slip.

Can a website look good and still hurt my business?

Yes. A site can look polished and still be slow, confusing on a phone or unclear about what to do next. Looks help, but they are only part of whether a site works for the people using it.

What should I do if my website is not getting enquiries?

Start with the basics: does it load quickly, work well on a phone and show a clear way to get in touch? Then ask a few customers to try it and tell you where they got stuck. Fix the biggest obstacle first rather than changing everything at once.

Do I need a new website or can mine be fixed?

Often the problems can be fixed without starting again, such as clearer wording, a visible contact button or smaller images. A rebuild makes sense when the foundations are the problem, for example a site that can't be edited or can't work well on phones. A good designer will tell you honestly which it is.
