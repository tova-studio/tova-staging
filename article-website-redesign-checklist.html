What do I need to get ready before a website redesign?

By the Tova Digital team · 19 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Before a website redesign, get clear on your goal, gather your words and photos, find your domain and hosting logins, and collect a few competitor sites you admire. Also decide what to keep from your current site. Having these ready saves time and cost, and helps any designer quote and start properly.

Most of the friction in a redesign has nothing to do with design. It's the hunt for a password, the photo nobody can find, or the discovery halfway through that no one agreed what the site is for.

This article is written by Tova Digital, a web design studio in Ōtautahi Christchurch with design and engineering under one roof. It works whoever you hire, and there's a printable checklist near the end you can hand to any designer.

Why does preparing before a redesign save time and cost?

Because every question a designer has to chase is time someone is paying for, and every gap found late costs more to fix than a gap found early. A little preparation moves the surprises to the start, when they're cheap.

It also makes your brief better. A designer who knows your goal, your customers and what you already have can give you a clearer quote and a more honest plan. If you're weighing up budget, our article on what a small business website costs in NZ is a good companion to this one.

None of it needs to be perfect. Rough and honest beats polished and late.

What goals should you set before a redesign?

Start with one clear sentence about what the new website should do for your business. Everything else, from the layout to the words, follows from it.

Good goals are about people doing things, not about the site looking nice. For example, you might want more phone calls, more booking requests, more quote enquiries or fewer repeat questions that your team answers by hand.

It also helps to write down what's frustrating you about the current site. Is it slow, hard to update, awkward on a phone, or just not you any more? Those frustrations are a very useful brief. If you're not sure how bad things are, this article on spotting a website that's hurting your business can help you name the problems.

What content should you gather first?

Gather the words that explain who you are, what you offer, who it's for and how to get in touch. These are the pieces a site can't be finished without, and they're the ones most often left until last.

Start with what already exists, and don't worry about the quality yet.

  • Your pages today. A simple list of every page on the current site and what each one is for.
  • Your services or products. What you offer, in the words your customers use.
  • Your story. A short paragraph on who you are and why customers choose you.
  • Reviews and testimonials. Written reviews, with the customer's permission to use them.
  • The questions you're always asked. These make a very good FAQ page.
  • Contact details. Address, phone, email, opening hours and service areas, checked and up to date.

A rough draft is plenty to begin with. A good designer can shape the layout around it, and the words can be refined as you go.

What photos and brand files do you need?

You need your logo in its original files, plus real photos of your work, your place and your people. Real photos almost always beat stock ones, because customers can tell the difference.

Before you start, gather these:

  • Your logo as an original file, not a screenshot or something saved from your old site.
  • Your brand colours and fonts, if you have them written down anywhere.
  • The best photos of your work, premises, products and team.
  • Confirmation that you have permission to use each photo, and who took it.

If your photos are dated or thin, say so early. A short photo session can lift a whole site, and it's much easier to plan for at the start than to squeeze in at the end.

Which logins and domain details should you find?

Find the login for your domain name, your hosting and your business email before the project begins. These are the details that most often go missing, and a redesign can stall waiting for them.

Your domain name is your web address, such as the .co.nz you've registered. It's held with a registrar, and it should be registered in your business's name, not a former designer's. Hosting is where the website files live. Business email may be separate again.

Here's what to track down:

  • The registrar login for your domain name, and who is listed as the owner.
  • The hosting login, or the name of the company that hosts the site today.
  • Access to your email provider, in case settings need to change.
  • Your Google Business Profile, analytics and social media accounts, if you have them.

If you can't find something, don't panic. Check old emails and ask whoever built the last site. Most registrars can help you prove you own a domain.

Which competitors and websites should you collect?

Collect a handful of websites you admire, from your industry or outside it, and write down what you like about each one. This tells a designer your taste far faster than a description does.

Be specific. "I like how clear their prices are" or "it loads fast and feels calm" is far more useful than "it looks modern". Include a competitor or two as well, so you can talk about what you'd do differently.

The point isn't to copy anyone. It's to give your designer a sense of what feels right, and what feels wrong, for your customers.

What should you keep from your old website?

Keep whatever is already working: the pages people visit, the words customers respond to and the reviews that build trust. A redesign doesn't mean starting from nothing.

Look at your analytics, if you have them, to see which pages get visited and which are ignored. Then sort your current site into three piles:

  1. Keep. Pages and wording that work and are still true.
  2. Improve. Pages that matter but are out of date, thin or hard to follow.
  3. Let go. Pages that are outdated, duplicated or no longer relevant.

Pages that already bring you visitors need care. If their addresses change, your designer should make sure the old ones point to the new ones, so you don't lose people or your place in Google. Your printable website redesign checklist

Print this page, tick things off as you go and hand it to whoever you hire. Anything you can't tick yet is simply a good question to ask at your first conversation.

  • ☐ I can say in one sentence what the new site should do.
  • ☐ I've listed what frustrates me about the current site.
  • ☐ I have a list of every page on the current site.
  • ☐ I know which pages to keep, improve or let go.
  • ☐ I have draft words for who we are, what we offer and how to contact us.
  • ☐ I've collected reviews or testimonials, with permission to use them.
  • ☐ I have our logo as an original file.
  • ☐ I've gathered our best photos and know we can use them.
  • ☐ I know where our domain is registered and have the login.
  • ☐ I know who hosts the current site and have the login.
  • ☐ I have access to our analytics, Google Business Profile and social accounts.
  • ☐ I've saved a few websites I admire, and a note on why.
  • ☐ I've listed a competitor or two to talk about.
  • ☐ One person has the final say on decisions.
  • ☐ I know when I'd like to be live, and why.

So what should you do next?

Start with your goal and your logins this week, because those two unlock the most. Then work through the rest of the checklist at your own pace.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to have everything ready before I contact a web designer?

No. Most people start with a rough idea and a few gaps, and a good designer will help you fill them. The checklist is there so you can answer the obvious questions quickly, not so you have to finish it first.

What if I can't find my domain or hosting logins?

It happens a lot, especially if a previous designer set things up. Check old emails for the registrar or hosting company, and ask your accountant or former developer. If you still can't find them, the registrar can usually help you prove you own the domain.

Should I write all the new content myself?

You know your business best, so a rough draft from you is a great starting point. You can also ask a designer or copywriter to help tidy it up. Just agree early who is responsible for the words, so nothing is left until the last minute.

Will I lose my Google rankings if I redesign my website?

Not if the redesign is handled carefully. Keep the pages that already bring you visitors, and make sure old addresses point to their new homes. Ask your designer how they plan to look after this before the project starts.
